Quantity, Curiosity, Depth

A rough model for the three skills that seem to matter most in research, and why it is surprisingly common to develop them in the wrong order.

Last updated: June 23, 2026

A successful researcher usually needs at least three things: quantity, curiosity, and depth. Quantity is the ability to generate attempts, iterate, write, test, and move. Curiosity is the habit of genuinely asking, looking around, and noticing what is strange or interesting. Depth is the ability to stay with one object for long enough to actually understand it, not just decorate it. These three qualities are all valuable, but they do not naturally come together. Many people are strong in one, decent in a second, and badly underdeveloped in the third.

The hard part is that depth is the most visibly intellectual one, so it gets romanticized. But in practice, some of the deepest people are still very hard to rely on in research jobs because they never built quantity or curiosity strongly enough. They can go deep only after someone else has already picked the target and created the motion around it. My impression is that the strongest long-run PhD prospects usually build the first two quite early, often before the PhD starts, and then learn real depth later. My own path was more uneven. During my master's I developed a bit of quantity and curiosity, then during the first three or four years of the PhD I mainly developed depth, and only later realized I had to rebuild the first two instead of assuming they would come back automatically.

That rebuilding is its own skill. After a long slow period, output does not return just because the bottleneck has become intellectually clear. Quantity has to be trained again almost mechanically: sending things, drafting before they are elegant, keeping more projects warm, and letting imperfect motion produce new curiosity. This is uncomfortable if depth became the only protected identity. But without rebuilding output, depth can turn into a private object rather than something useful to collaborators, students, or the broader research process.

Open questions for later
  • Is quantity mostly habit, environment, or emotional stability?
  • Does curiosity decay when every question starts feeling expensive?
  • Can depth be taught directly, or only protected long enough to appear?
